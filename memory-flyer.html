Burlington, North Carolina — 1984

When Memory Takes the Stand

What decades of DNA exonerations reveal about eyewitness identification, and what it means for the judges and juries who rely on it.

The case that changed how North Carolina runs a lineup

In July 1984, a college student was raped in her Burlington apartment. Determined to survive and help convict her attacker, she studied his face throughout the assault, promising herself she would remember every detail.

Days later she picked Ronald Cotton from a photo lineup, then again from a physical lineup. At trial she testified with total confidence. Cotton was convicted and sentenced to life plus fifty years.

Eleven years later, DNA testing proved she had identified the wrong man. Her memory hadn't lied out of malice — it had simply done what memories do: filled gaps, absorbed new information, and grown more confident even as it grew less accurate.

What a false memory actually is

  • Memory isn't a recording that gets played back. It's rebuilt each time it's recalled — and every rebuild is a chance for it to shift.
  • Information encountered after an event — a leading question, a detective's comment, a photo seen later — can blend into the original memory without the person noticing the seam.
  • Confidence is not a measure of accuracy. A witness can feel completely certain about a detail that is completely wrong.
  • Psychologist Elizabeth Loftus has shown that people shown an altered version of an image afterward will often adopt the altered version as their memory, discarding the accurate one entirely.
Exhibit B

Why eyewitness testimony goes wrong

  • Stress narrows attention. Under threat — a knife, an assault — attention often locks onto the danger itself, leaving less capacity to encode the surrounding face.
  • Real recognition is fast. Psychologist Gary Wells has found that genuine identifications typically take seconds; a witness who deliberates for minutes, comparing photo against photo, is often doing something other than simple recognition.
  • Feedback inflates confidence. In controlled studies, witnesses who picked the wrong person and were then told "you got the suspect" became dramatically more confident and recalled a clearer view of the crime — though nothing about the actual memory had changed.
  • When the real perpetrator isn't in a lineup, witnesses tend to pick whoever looks closest to their memory — not no one.

What's at stake for the justice system

  • Eyewitness misidentification has been a factor in the large majority of the country's DNA exonerations — by some counts, more than three in four.
  • A confident witness pointing across a courtroom remains some of the most persuasive evidence a jury will ever see, even though confidence at trial — often expressed after months or years of retelling — says little about accuracy at the moment of the original identification.
  • Wrongful convictions carry a second victim: Ronald Cotton served roughly eleven years for a crime he didn't commit, while the actual assailant remained free.

Practical guidance for the bench and jury box

  • Ask how the identification was collected. Was it administered by someone who didn't know who the suspect was ("blind" administration)? Were photos shown one at a time ("sequential") rather than side by side?
  • Weigh certainty at the time of the identification, not certainty at trial. Confidence tends to grow with each retelling and every piece of confirming feedback.
  • Ask what happened between the identification and the testimony — news coverage, conversations with police, repeated interviews. Any of these can reshape what a witness remembers seeing.
  • Treat memory as reconstructed, not recorded — for every witness, in every case, regardless of how sincere or certain they appear.
